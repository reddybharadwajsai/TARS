# Chapter 3: Physical Processes of the Coast

The coastline is shaped by a continuous interplay of constructive and destructive forces. Waves, tides, currents, wind, and geological processes work together over timescales ranging from seconds to millennia, sculpting the diverse landforms we observe along the world's shorelines. Understanding these physical processes is essential for predicting how coastlines will respond to climate change and human modification.

## Wave Dynamics

Ocean waves are the primary agent of coastal change. Generated by wind acting on the sea surface, waves transmit energy across entire ocean basins before releasing it along coastlines. The energy delivered by waves depends on their height, period, and angle of approach — parameters that vary with weather patterns, season, and coastal orientation.

When waves enter shallow water, they undergo transformation through processes of shoaling, refraction, and breaking. Shoaling causes wave height to increase as water depth decreases. Refraction bends wave crests toward alignment with depth contours, concentrating energy on headlands and dispersing it in bays. Breaking releases wave energy as turbulence, generating currents and suspending sediment for transport.

The distinction between constructive and destructive waves is fundamental to understanding beach morphology. Low-energy swell waves with long periods tend to push sediment onshore, building beaches upward and seaward. High-energy storm waves with short periods pull sediment offshore, eroding beaches and depositing material in nearshore bars. The seasonal alternation between these wave types produces the familiar cycle of summer beach accretion and winter erosion.

## Tidal Processes

Tides are the predictable rise and fall of sea level caused primarily by the gravitational attraction of the moon and sun. Tidal range — the vertical difference between high and low water — varies from negligible in some enclosed seas to over fifteen meters in funnel-shaped embayments. The magnitude of tidal range profoundly influences the character of coastal environments and the organisms that inhabit them.

Tidal currents generated by the horizontal movement of water during flood and ebb tides are significant agents of sediment transport in estuaries, tidal inlets, and along open coastlines. In constricted channels, tidal currents can reach velocities sufficient to move cobbles and small boulders, creating distinctive bedforms and sediment patterns.

The interaction between tides and river flow in estuaries creates complex patterns of water circulation and sediment distribution. The mixing of fresh and salt water drives density-driven currents that trap fine sediment within the estuary, building the mudflats and salt marshes that characterize these environments.

## Coastal Erosion and Sediment Transport

Coastal erosion is the removal of material from the shoreline by wave action, currents, and other processes. While often viewed negatively due to its impacts on human infrastructure, erosion is a natural process that supplies sediment to beaches, dunes, and offshore environments. The challenge for coastal management is distinguishing natural erosion cycles from accelerated erosion caused by human intervention or climate change.

Longshore transport — the movement of sediment parallel to the coast — is driven by waves approaching the shoreline at an angle. This process creates a river of sand flowing along the coast, building spits, filling embayments, and redistributing sediment between erosion and deposition zones. Interruption of longshore transport by structures such as groins, jetties, and breakwaters commonly causes accretion on the updrift side and erosion on the downdrift side.

## Sea Level Change

Sea level rise represents the most pervasive threat to coastlines worldwide. Current rates of global mean sea level rise, approximately three to four millimeters per year, reflect contributions from thermal expansion of warming ocean water, melting of mountain glaciers, and mass loss from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. Projections for the twenty-first century range from approximately half a meter to over a meter of additional rise, depending on future greenhouse gas emissions.

The impacts of sea level rise include increased flooding, accelerated erosion, saltwater intrusion into coastal aquifers, and the gradual inundation of low-lying areas. These impacts interact with other stressors including storm intensification, changes in river discharge, and continued coastal development to create compound risks for coastal communities and ecosystems.
